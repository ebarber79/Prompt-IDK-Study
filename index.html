Definition

What an IDK is

An IDK is the smallest part of a prompt you can change on its own and get a different result. Not a sentence, not a "technique" — a single tunable unit.

The reason to name them is diagnostic. Prompts fail as a whole, so the instinct is to rewrite the whole thing, and the rewrite changes six units at once. You get a different answer and learn nothing about which unit was carrying the failure. Naming the unit turns a rewrite into an experiment.

Once you have units, you can sort them, and they sort cleanly into three questions: where does this sit, what am I asking for, and how tightly is it pinned. That is the whole framework.

The claim this guide is organised around

Most bad prompts are not badly written. They are missing a family. A prompt with rich Detail and no Action wanders; one with a crisp Action and no Detail returns something technically responsive and useless; one with both but wrong Location gets read in the wrong order or with the wrong authority and quietly loses. Before you rewrite, name the missing family.

Core framework

Three families

L

Location

Where a unit sits — which slot, how deep in the context, next to what, inside which boundary.

asks: where does this go?
A

Action

What you are actually asking for — the verb, its output contract, and when to stop.

asks: what should happen?
D

Detail

How tightly the ask is pinned — constraints, demonstrations, rubrics, sources.

asks: how precisely?

The families are not ranked. They are independent axes, which is the useful property: you can hold two fixed and move the third, and that is what every drill in this guide does.

Technical grounding

Where each family has real evidence — and where it doesn't

Location / Action / Detail is a framing, not a research result. The units underneath vary a lot in how well they are evidenced, so each one is labelled. Three tiers: measured (published result), vendor guidance (documented by a model provider), folklore (widely practised, mechanism plausible, no citation I trust).

Anchors, by family

Location — measured, and strongly
Where relevant information sits in a long context changes accuracy, in a U-shape: facts at the start or end are used well, facts in the middle are used badly. Liu et al., 2023 — "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts". Order effects are just as sharp at small scale: permuting few-shot examples can move a model "between near state-of-the-art and random guess" performance (Lu et al., 2021), and models carry a positional bias toward later answers (Zhao et al., 2021 — "Calibrate Before Use") and toward particular option letters regardless of content (Zheng et al., 2023).
Location — vendor guidance
Anthropic's prompting docs say to put long documents above the query, instructions, and examples, and state that queries at the end "can improve response quality by up to 30 percent in tests, especially with complex, multidocument inputs." Same page recommends XML tags to make the seams between instructions, context, examples, and input unambiguous. Anthropic — prompting best practices.
Action — measured, narrowly
The one solid result here is about polarity: models handle negated instructions poorly, and get worse at it as they scale — an inverse scaling result across pretrained, instruction-tuned, few-shot and fine-tuned variants. Jang et al., 2022 — "Can Large Language Models Truly Understand Prompts? A Case Study with Negated Prompts". This is why "tell the model what to do instead of what not to do" appears verbatim in vendor guidance. The rest of the Action family — verb choice, role vs instruction, stop conditions — is craft with weak published backing. Labelled honestly below.
Detail — measured, and counterintuitively
Demonstrations do not teach what people assume. Randomly corrupting the labels in few-shot examples barely hurts performance; what the examples actually supply is the label space, the input distribution, and the output format. Min et al., 2022 — "Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations". Practical consequence: examples are a format instrument first and a correctness instrument second.
What has no anchor
Restating a constraint at the end of a prompt ("sandwiching"), rubric-before-task, and most verb-choice advice are folklore in the precise sense: I use them, the mechanism is plausible given the position results above, and I could not find a citation I would defend. They are marked folklore in the catalog rather than dressed up.

The catalog

18 units, three families

Each unit: what it is, the knob you turn, the failure signature to recognise it by, and an A/B drill you can run in ten minutes. Mark them off as you actually run them — reading this list does nothing.

Location

Where the unit sits

Six units. This is the family with the best evidence and the least attention — almost all popular prompt advice is Action and Detail.

L1

Slot

measured · vendor guidance

The same sentence carries different weight depending on whether it arrives as a system prompt, a user turn, an assistant prefill, or a tool result. Slot sets authority and persistence — a system-slot rule survives twenty turns; the same rule typed in turn three decays.

Knob: move the sentence between slots without changing a word.

Failure signature The model obeys for two turns and then drifts back. That is almost never a wording problem; it is a durable rule sitting in a disposable slot.

Drill

Take a standing constraint you keep having to repeat.

A — leave it in the user turn where you normally type it. B — move it verbatim into the system prompt.

Run a 10-turn conversation each way. Count the turn number where the constraint is first violated.

Done when: you have a turn-number for A and a turn-number for B.

L2

Depth

measured — Liu et al. 2023

How far into a long context the needed information sits. Accuracy follows a U-shape: strong at the start, strong at the end, weakest in the middle. Vendor guidance converges on the same shape from the other direction — long documents at the top, the actual query last, "up to 30 percent" better on multidocument inputs.

Knob: position of the payload relative to the query — not its presence.

Failure signature The model misses a fact that is demonstrably in the context. You paste the fact on its own and it answers correctly. Nothing about the fact changed except its depth.

Drill

Build a context of ~8 documents where document 5 holds the answer.

A — ask the question first, then paste the documents. B — paste the documents first, ask the question last. C — as B, but move the answer-bearing document to position 1.

Three runs, same question. The gap between B and C is the depth penalty on your model.

Done when: you can state your model's depth penalty as a rough percentage, not a belief.

L3

Adjacency

folklore — mechanism follows from L2

Whether an instruction sits next to the data it governs, or in a rules block far away from it. "Summarise each section in one line" at the top of a 40-section document is a different unit from the same sentence repeated beside each section.

Knob: distance between a rule and the thing it applies to.

Failure signature The rule is applied correctly to the first few items and abandoned partway down a long list.

Drill

Take a per-item transformation over 20+ items.

A — state the rule once, up top. B — state it once up top and inline beside each item.

Count items processed correctly before the first deviation.

Done when: you know whether adjacency buys anything on your task, or nothing.

L4

Boundary

vendor guidance

The seams that tell the model where one kind of content stops and another starts — XML tags, fences, headers. Without them, instructions and data are one undifferentiated blob and the model has to guess which is which. Wrapping each kind in its own tag (<instructions>, <context>, <input>) is the documented fix.

Knob: whether the seams are marked, and how consistently.

Failure signature The model executes something that was inside your data as if it were an instruction — or treats your instruction as content to be summarised. Both are boundary failures, and the second is the tell that gets missed.

Drill

Take a prompt that mixes instructions with a pasted document containing imperative sentences.

A — run it flat, no tags. B — wrap the document in <document> and the ask in <instructions>.

Look specifically for whether any imperative from inside the document leaked into the behaviour.

Done when: you have seen a boundary failure happen at least once, deliberately.

L5

Order

measured — Lu et al. 2021 · Zhao et al. 2021 · Zheng et al. 2023 · local null 2026-07-29

The sequence of interchangeable items: few-shot examples, candidate options, ranked lists. Treated as cosmetic, measured as decisive — reordering examples alone has moved classification accuracy from near-SOTA to near-chance. Models also carry standing biases: toward later answers, and toward particular option letters independent of content.

Knob: permutation of an unordered set.

Failure signature Two runs that differ only in example order disagree. If you have never checked, you do not know whether your prompt's performance is a property of the prompt or of one lucky ordering.

Drill

Take any few-shot prompt with 4+ examples.

A — your current order. B–E — four random permutations, everything else identical.

Run the same 20 test inputs through all five. Report two numbers: the accuracy spread and the count of individual items whose label changed. They can disagree — see below.

Field note — drill run 2026-07-29 · null result

Ran exactly as written: llama-3.1-8b-instant, temperature 0, 8 balanced examples, 20 deliberately subtle held-out reviews, five orderings. Accuracy spread: 0.0 points. All five orderings scored 16/20. A sixth arm repeated ordering A and returned byte-identical predictions, so the null is real and not a determinism artefact.

Only 1/20 items varied with ordering at all — and that variation was between two wrong answers, so it never touched the score. The published effect is real; it did not show up here, at this scale, on this task.

What the run changed about this card: the drill originally said accuracy spread was "your prompt's real error bar." That is wrong as a sole metric — spread can be exactly zero while the model's actual outputs still move. Per-item flip count is the more sensitive instrument and is now part of the drill.

Done when: you can quote both a spread and a flip count, and you know whether your headline number was a lucky draw.

L6

Recurrence

folklore — consistent with L2

Restating the load-bearing constraint at the end, after the payload. Cheap, widely practised, and mechanically plausible given that the end of the context is a strong position — but I have no citation isolating the effect, so it is marked folklore rather than sold as a result.

Knob: one repetition of one constraint, at the tail.

Failure signature Long prompts where the output is good but violates the single rule you cared about most.

Drill

Pick a long prompt with one non-negotiable constraint.

A — constraint stated once, up front. B — identical, plus one restatement as the final line.

20 runs each. Count violations. If the delta is zero on your task, drop the habit — that is a real result too.

Done when: you have a violation count for A and B, and have decided whether to keep the habit.

Action

What you are asking for

Six units. The most-written-about family and the least-measured — most of what follows is craft, labelled as such.

A1

The verb

folklore

Extract, summarise, classify, rewrite, critique, decide — each selects a different behaviour, and they are not interchangeable. "Look at this code" is not a verb; it is a gesture at one. Most vague outputs trace back to a prompt whose main clause has no imperative in it at all.

Knob: the single imperative your prompt is built around.

Failure signature The response is a competent essay about your input rather than an operation performed on it.

Drill

Take your last five real prompts. Underline the main verb in each. For any that have none, or have three competing ones, rewrite to exactly one and re-run.

Done when: five prompts audited, and you know your own default failure — missing verb or competing verbs.

A2

Role vs instruction

folklore — contested

"You are a senior security engineer" and "find the auth bypasses in this diff" are different units doing different jobs. Role sets register, vocabulary, and what the model treats as salient. Instruction sets what comes back. Role is widely over-used as a substitute for instruction, which is the failure worth naming.

Knob: presence of a persona, independent of the ask.

Failure signature Output that sounds like the persona and does not do the task. A security-flavoured summary instead of a list of bypasses.

Drill

A — role only, no explicit ask. B — explicit ask only, no role. C — both.

Same input three ways. The interesting comparison is B vs C: does the role add anything once the ask is sharp?

Done when: you can say what role buys you on top of a sharp instruction, on your task.

A3

Polarity

measured — Jang et al. 2022

Whether the instruction says do-this or don't-do-that. Negated instructions are followed measurably worse, and the effect gets worse with scale — an inverse scaling result that held across pretrained, instruction-tuned, few-shot and fine-tuned models. Vendor guidance says the same thing as a flat rule: tell the model what to do instead of what not to do.

Knob: rewrite each prohibition as the positive behaviour that replaces it.

Failure signature The exact thing you forbade shows up in the output. Reading the prompt back, the forbidden thing is the most concrete noun in it.

Drill

Find a prompt with three or more "don't" clauses.

A — as written. B — every prohibition converted to a positive instruction, same intent.

20 runs each, count violations of the original three rules.

Done when: you have converted one real prompt end-to-end and measured the difference.

A4

Output contract

craft — mechanically enforceable · measured here 2026-07-29

The shape the answer must arrive in: a schema, a fixed set of fields, a single token. This is an Action unit, not a Detail one — the distinction matters because a contract can be enforced (structured outputs, tool calls, a parser that rejects) rather than requested. Requesting JSON and enforcing JSON are different units with different failure rates.

Knob: requested vs enforced, and how tight the shape is.

Failure signature Valid content wrapped in prose you have to strip. Or worse: it parses nine times out of ten, so the failure only appears in production.

Drill

A — "return JSON with fields x, y, z". B — the same, enforced via structured output or a tool schema.

100 runs each if you can afford it, and count parse failures. The rate in A is the number people quote as "basically always works".

Field note — measured 2026-07-29 · found by accident

This number came out of running the L5 drill, not this one. The classifier prompt there said: "Reply with exactly one word: POSITIVE or NEGATIVE." On 3/20 items — 15% — the model replied NEUTRAL, a label that appears nowhere in the instruction or in any of the eight examples.

All three were genuinely borderline reviews ("It's fine. I guess. For the price."). Faced with an input that fits neither offered label, the model invented a third rather than picking the closer one. A requested contract has no mechanism to prevent that; an enforced one does.

Worth noting where it was found: a drill aimed at one family surfaced a larger effect in another. Zero-point spread on the unit under test, 15% contract violation sitting underneath it.

Done when: you have a parse-failure rate for the requested version, in numbers.

A5

Reasoning placement

measured for existence · craft for placement

Whether the model is allowed to work before answering, and where that work is allowed to live — extended thinking, a <thinking> block, a plan-then-do structure, or nowhere. That step-by-step reasoning helps on multi-step problems is well established; where you put it is craft, and it interacts with the output contract in A4 — reasoning that lands inside your JSON is a bug.

Knob: permitted, and separated from the answer, or not.

Failure signature Either confident wrong answers on multi-step tasks (no room to work), or working-out leaking into a machine-read field (room, but unseparated).

Drill

On a task needing 3+ steps:

A — answer only. B — reason then answer, both in the response. C — reason in a separate channel, answer alone in the contract.

Score accuracy and parse-cleanliness separately. They move in different directions.

Done when: you have both numbers and can see the tension between them.

A6

Termination

craft — load-bearing for agents

When to stop, and what to do when the task cannot be completed. "If the file does not exist, say so and stop" is an action you specified. Without it, the model supplies its own stop condition, and its default is to produce something. Abstention is a specified action or it does not happen.

Knob: presence of an explicit stop/abstain branch.

Failure signature Plausible output about a thing that does not exist. Confident answers to questions the context cannot support. An agent that keeps going after the useful work is done.

Drill

Ask a question your context genuinely cannot answer.

A — no abstention clause. B — "if the context does not contain the answer, reply exactly: NOT IN CONTEXT."

10 unanswerable questions each. Count fabrications.

Done when: you have a fabrication count with and without the branch.

Detail

How tightly it is pinned

Six units. The family people reach for first, and the one with the sharpest own-goal at the end.

D1

Specificity gradient

craft

Detail is not binary, it is a slider, and the useful move is knowing where on the slider you are. "Make it better" → "make it shorter" → "cut to 120 words, drop adverbs, keep paragraph two intact." Each step removes a decision from the model and hands it to you. That is a cost as well as a gain.

Knob: how many decisions you have taken back.

Failure signature Two symptoms, opposite causes — output that misses your intent (too far left) or output that is exactly what you asked for and worse than what the model would have done unprompted (too far right).

Drill

Same task at three settings: one line of guidance, one paragraph, one full rubric. Rank the three outputs blind. The winner is your calibration point for this task type — and it is often not the most specific one.

Done when: you have found one task where less detail won.

D2

Demonstration

measured — Min et al. 2022

Detail delivered by example rather than description. The counterintuitive finding: corrupting the labels in your few-shot examples barely dents performance. What demonstrations actually supply is the label space, the input distribution, and the output format. Examples are a format instrument first, a correctness instrument second — which reframes the usual advice to agonise over picking perfect examples.

Knob: count, diversity, and format-fidelity of examples — in roughly that order of leverage.

Failure signature Output that is correct in substance and wrong in shape, when you have described the shape in prose instead of showing it once.

Drill

A — three examples, correct labels. B — three examples, labels deliberately shuffled, format identical. C — zero examples, format described in prose.

Replicating the published result on your own task is the point. If B ≈ A and both beat C, examples are doing format work, not teaching.

Field note — 2026-07-29 · a limit on the claim

"Demonstrations supply the label space" is the published finding, and it is easy to over-read as guarantee the label space. In our own run, eight examples using only POSITIVE and NEGATIVE still left the model emitting NEUTRAL on 15% of borderline inputs (see A4).

So: demonstrations establish a label space, they do not enforce one. If the space must hold, that is an Action-family job, not a Detail-family one.

Done when: you have run the label-corruption arm yourself and seen what it does on your task.

D3

Counterexample

folklore — tension with A3

Showing what a bad answer looks like, not just a good one. Sits in genuine tension with A3 (polarity): a counterexample is a prohibition delivered by demonstration, and the negation research suggests prohibitions are followed badly. Whether showing beats telling for negatives is exactly the kind of question this catalog exists to make testable.

Knob: negative examples present or absent, and whether labelled as such.

Failure signature The model reproduces the counterexample. This is the specific risk and it is not rare.

Drill

A — positive examples only. B — positives plus one clearly-labelled negative.

Count how often B's output resembles the negative. This is an open question, not a settled technique — record what you find.

Done when: you have a rate for "reproduced the counterexample" on your own task.

D4

Rubric

folklore

Stating what "good" means before the work, rather than judging after. A rubric is Detail that constrains the evaluation rather than the output, and it is the unit that makes self-checking possible at all — a model cannot check its work against a standard you never wrote down.

Knob: criteria stated up front vs applied afterwards vs never written.

Failure signature You know the output is wrong but cannot say why in one sentence. That is a missing rubric, not a bad output.

Drill

Before your next real prompt, write the three criteria you will judge the result by. Then include them in the prompt.

A — criteria kept to yourself. B — criteria stated in the prompt.

Judge both against the same three criteria you wrote first.

Done when: you have written criteria before seeing an output at least once.

D5

Grounding

craft — architectural

Detail that comes from outside the model: pasted sources, retrieved documents, tool results, quotes the answer must be built from. The unit is not "add context" — it is requiring the answer to be traceable to it. Grounding without a traceability requirement is just more tokens.

Knob: sources present, and whether citation of them is mandatory.

Failure signature An answer consistent with the sources but not derived from them — right for the wrong reasons, and it will be wrong the next time the sources change.

Drill

A — sources provided, answer requested. B — sources provided, every claim must quote the source line it came from.

Then plant one false fact in the sources. B should surface it; A will usually route around it using prior knowledge — which is the failure, even though the answer looks better.

Done when: you have run the planted-fact test and seen A route around it.

D6

Over-constraint

observed — own experiment, 2026-07-26

The failure mode of this whole family: two details that each look reasonable, cannot both be satisfied by the obvious implementation, and the model silently picks one. No error, no flag — just a result that quietly drops a requirement while reporting success.

The case this unit comes from

Two agents worked in parallel on the same display function from separate specs. One had to make divide 10 3 print 3.33; the other had to make multiply 5000 400 print 2,000,000.0. Each wrote the obvious single format spec — .2f for one, :, for the other — and each silently broke the other's requirement.

The requirements were never actually contradictory: f"{round(value, 2):,}" satisfies both. What conflicted was the two implementations, and neither agent could see the other's. The lesson generalises past agents — a single prompt with two constraints that no obvious single mechanism satisfies produces the same silent drop, and you will not be told which constraint lost.

Knob: whether any two of your constraints are jointly satisfiable by an obvious mechanism — checked before you run, not after.

Failure signature Output satisfies most of your requirements. Nothing announces which one it dropped. You notice weeks later.

Drill

Take a prompt with five or more constraints. Before running it, ask a model to list any pairs that cannot both hold, and to name a mechanism satisfying each pair. Then run it and check the dropped ones.

Done when: you have caught one silent drop before it reached an output.

Image track

The same three families, pointed at images

Location / Action / Detail is also, almost word for word, the standard anatomy of an image prompt. That is a useful coincidence and a slightly misleading one — worth stating plainly before using it.

The asymmetry, stated up front

In text, Location means position in the context window — an attention property. In images, it means position in the frame — a compositional property. Same word, two different referents. The mapping below is a genuine structural parallel, not one mechanism appearing twice, and treating it as the latter will mislead you.

I·L

Location — the frame

image · craft

Setting, camera position, framing, and where the subject sits within the composition. "A lighthouse" versus "a lighthouse in the lower-left third, shot from sea level, storm filling the upper frame." Aspect ratio belongs here too — it changes what the model composes, not just how the result is cropped.

Knob: scene, camera placement, subject position in frame, aspect ratio.

Failure signature A correct subject, centred and floating, with no scene around it — the default composition, which is the tell that you specified no Location at all.

Drill

Same subject and style, three framings: eye level / low angle / overhead. Then the same three at a different aspect ratio. Six images. Note which changes altered the content, not just the crop.

Done when: you have a case where aspect ratio changed what appeared in the image.

I·A

Action — the verb, and whose it is

image / video · craft

What the subject is doing. Static subjects are the default, so any motion has to be specified. In video this unit splits in two and the split is the whole game: subject motion and camera motion are separate actions, and prompts that conflate them produce the classic failure where everything drifts at once.

Knob: subject verb, and — for video — camera verb, specified separately.

Failure signature Stills: a posed, inert subject. Video: the whole scene sliding because you asked for movement without saying whose.

Drill

A — subject moving, camera locked off. B — subject still, camera moving. C — both, named separately in one prompt.

Whether C holds the separation is a real property of the model you are using.

Done when: you know whether your video model keeps subject and camera motion separate when told to.

I·D

Detail — style, light, material

image · craft

Lighting, lens, material, palette, medium, render fidelity. The family where over-constraint (D6) bites hardest and most visibly: stack twelve style words and the model averages them into mush, and no error tells you which four it ignored.

Knob: number and mutual compatibility of style descriptors.

Failure signature A generic "AI look" despite a long, specific prompt. That is the averaging, and the fix is subtraction rather than more words.

Drill

Take a prompt with 10+ style descriptors. Generate it. Then cut to the three you would keep and generate again. Then cut to one. Pick the winner blind — and note that this is D1's specificity gradient, run in a different medium.

Done when: a three-word style prompt has beaten your ten-word one at least once.

Provenance

What is borrowed, what is mine, what is unproven

Borrowed: every result in the grounding section, cited inline and linked to the primary source. The vendor guidance is Anthropic's published prompting documentation, quoted rather than paraphrased where a number is involved.

Mine: the Location / Action / Detail split itself, the unit list underneath it, the failure signatures, and the drills. None of that is a research result — it is a filing system, and its only defence is that it makes failures nameable and differences testable.

Field notes: three units (L5, A4, D2) carry a dated field note from a drill actually run against a live model. One of them is a null — the published order-sensitivity effect did not reproduce at our scale, and the card says so instead of quietly dropping the run. Field notes record what the run changed about the card, including when it proved the drill's own metric inadequate.

Unproven, and marked: six of the eighteen text units carry folklore or craft rather than a citation. That is not a gap to be filled with a citation that nearly fits — it is the backlog. Each drill is written so its unit can be moved from folklore to measured by running it, which is the point of the format.
